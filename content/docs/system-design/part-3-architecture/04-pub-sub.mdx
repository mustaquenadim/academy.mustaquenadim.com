## The problem

A customer places an order. That one fact needs to ripple everywhere: charge the card, send
a confirmation email, decrement inventory, update the analytics dashboard, notify the
warehouse, award loyalty points. Today your Orders service calls each of those services
directly, one after another.

Every time the business adds a reaction — "also send an SMS," "also update the fraud model" —
someone edits the Orders service and redeploys it. Orders now imports and knows about eight
other services. It's slow (it waits on all of them), fragile (if the loyalty service is down,
the order fails), and the team that owns Orders has become a bottleneck for everyone who
wants to react to an order.

## A first attempt

Push the calls onto a message queue so Orders doesn't block. Better — Orders enqueues a job
and returns. But a plain queue is *point-to-point*: each message goes to exactly one
consumer. To feed six different reactions you'd create six queues and have Orders write the
same event into all six, one enqueue per consumer.

That's still Orders knowing its whole audience. Add a seventh reaction and Orders changes
again. The coupling didn't go away — you just moved it. The producer is still responsible for
knowing every consumer, which is exactly the knot you wanted to cut.

## The insight

Flip who knows whom. Instead of the producer sending to N known consumers, the producer
publishes one event to a named **topic** and forgets about it. Any service that cares
**subscribes** to that topic and receives its own copy.

That's **publish/subscribe**. Orders publishes `OrderPlaced` once. Email, Inventory,
Analytics, and Loyalty each subscribe independently. Adding a new reaction means adding a new
subscriber — *the producer never changes*. Orders no longer knows or cares who's listening.

<Callout type="idea" title="Pub/sub inverts the dependency">
In direct calls, the producer depends on every consumer. In pub/sub, consumers depend on the
*event*, not the producer. Producers and consumers evolve independently — the essence of
event-driven architecture.
</Callout>

## How it works

<Steps>

<Step>

### Producer publishes to a topic

Orders emits one message — `OrderPlaced { orderId, userId, total }` — to the `orders` topic.
It doesn't know how many subscribers exist, or any of them. Publish and move on.

</Step>

<Step>

### The broker fans out

The broker copies that event to every subscription attached to the topic. One publish becomes
N deliveries — one per interested service — without the producer lifting a finger.

</Step>

<Step>

### Each subscriber consumes independently

Email, Inventory, and Analytics each have their own subscription and their own backlog. A
slow subscriber builds up its own queue; it doesn't slow the others or the publisher. Each
processes at its own pace.

</Step>

<Step>

### Add reactions without touching the producer

Marketing wants to send an SMS on every order? They stand up an SMS service and subscribe to
`orders`. Orders is never redeployed, never even told. The system grows at the edges.

</Step>

</Steps>

```
                                 ┌──────────────▶  Email service
                                 │
   Orders ──publish──▶ ┌──────────┐ ──▶  Inventory service
   (OrderPlaced)       │  TOPIC   │
                       │  orders  │ ──▶  Analytics service
                       └──────────┘
                                 │
                                 └──────────────▶  Loyalty service
             one publish  ───────▶  fan-out to every subscriber
```

## Concrete numbers

Fan-out is the headline: 1 publish → N deliveries. A busy topic on Kafka or Google Pub/Sub
handles hundreds of thousands to millions of messages per second and delivers to each
subscriber group in parallel, typically within tens of milliseconds end to end.

Compare the coupling cost directly. Direct calls to 6 services in sequence, each ~20 ms, make
the order request wait ~120 ms and fail if any one is down. Publish to a topic and Orders
waits ~2 ms for the broker ack and *always* succeeds regardless of a subscriber being down —
that subscriber just processes its backlog once it recovers. You traded synchronous
certainty for asynchronous resilience.

## When to use it

<Callout type="warn" title="Fan-out means no single 'it's done' moment">
After Orders publishes, the email may go out in 50 ms and inventory may update 3 s later.
There's no instant where you can say "the whole order finished," because each subscriber runs
on its own clock. If a step *must* complete before you respond to the user (like charging the
card), keep that synchronous — publish only the reactions that are genuinely fire-and-forget.
</Callout>

<Callout type="error" title="Ordering and duplicates are still your problem">
Global ordering across a topic is usually not guaranteed — only within a partition/key. If
`OrderUpdated` can arrive before `OrderPlaced`, subscribers must handle it (partition by
`orderId` to keep one order's events in order). And like queues, pub/sub is at-least-once:
subscribers must be idempotent, because every subscriber can receive the same event twice.
</Callout>

## Practice

<Accordions>

<Accordion title="When do you reach for a queue versus pub/sub? They look almost identical.">

Ask: does this message need *one* handler or *many*? A queue is point-to-point — one message,
one consumer from the group — perfect for distributing work (one worker transcodes a given
video). Pub/sub is broadcast — one event, every subscriber gets a copy — perfect for
notifying many services of a fact (an order was placed). Rule of thumb: "do this task" →
queue; "this happened" → pub/sub. Many brokers combine them: a topic fans out, and each
subscription is itself a queue load-balanced across that service's workers.

</Accordion>

<Accordion title="A new subscriber is added six months in. How does it catch up on past events?">

Depends on the broker's retention model. An ephemeral bus (classic pub/sub) only delivers
events published *after* you subscribe — the new service sees the future, not the past. A
retained log like Kafka keeps events for a configured window (days, or forever with
compaction), so a new subscriber can replay from the beginning and rebuild its state. If
subscribers need to bootstrap from history, choose a log with retention; if they only care
about "what's happening now," an ephemeral bus is cheaper.

</Accordion>

<Accordion title="The Analytics subscriber is down for an hour. What happens to orders and to the other subscribers?">

Nothing, for the producer and the other subscribers. Orders keeps publishing, Email and
Inventory keep consuming — each subscription has its own independent backlog. Analytics' own
queue simply grows while it's down and drains when it recovers (assuming retention is long
enough to hold an hour of events). That per-subscriber isolation is the whole point: one slow
or dead consumer can't stall the publisher or its siblings, unlike the direct-call version
where a dead service failed the order.

</Accordion>

</Accordions>

## Recap

- Pub/sub inverts the dependency: producers publish one event to a topic and don't know their
  subscribers; consumers depend on the event, not the producer.
- One publish fans out to every subscriber, and each consumes on its own independent backlog —
  so you add reactions by adding subscribers, never by touching the producer.
- The costs are no single "done" moment, weak cross-topic ordering, and at-least-once
  delivery — keep must-complete steps synchronous and make subscribers idempotent.

## In an interview

<Callout type="info" title="How to discuss this">
Frame pub/sub as "this happened" events versus a queue's "do this task." The line that scores
points: the producer publishes once and doesn't know its subscribers, so new reactions are
added without redeploying the producer. Then volunteer the hard parts — no single completion
moment, ordering only within a partition key, and at-least-once duplicates — and say keep the
card charge synchronous while everything else reacts asynchronously.
</Callout>
